Should you prepay your loan or invest the extra? Real math, no opinions.

10 min read · Updated for 2026

Every personal finance forum eventually arrives at the same fight. One side says "always prepay your mortgage, debt is slavery." The other says "your loan is at 6%, the stock market returns 10%, invest the difference." Both sides are wrong half the time — because the answer depends on a variable they're skipping. Let's run the actual numbers.

The setup

You have a $200,000 mortgage at 6.5% interest, 30-year term. Your monthly payment is about $1,264. You've found an extra $500/month. Two options:

  • Option A — Prepay: Pay $500/month extra against the loan principal.
  • Option B — Invest: Put $500/month into an index fund returning 10% annually.

Use our loan calculator for option A and the investment calculator for option B as you read along.

Option A: Prepay the loan

Plug $200,000 / 6.5% / 30 years / $500 extra into the loan calculator. The result:

  • Loan paid in ~18 years 8 months instead of 30.
  • Time saved: ~11 years 4 months.
  • Interest saved: ~$108,000.

That's a guaranteed, risk-free return equivalent to your loan rate (6.5%). Once the loan is paid off, you have ~11 years left where the original $1,264 + $500 = $1,764/month is freed up. If you redirect that into the same index fund for those remaining 11 years and 4 months at 10%, you end up with roughly $410,000 in invested capital.

Option B: Invest the $500 instead

Plug $500/month for 30 years at 10% (no step-up) into the investment calculator. Result:

  • Total invested: $180,000.
  • Final corpus: ~$1,130,000.

But you also pay the loan in full over 30 years and pay roughly $255,000 in interest. So your net position is: $1,130,000 in investments minus the extra $75,000 of interest you paid (compared to the prepay scenario) = about $1,055,000 net.

So Option B wins by ~$640,000?

On paper, yes. In reality, that comparison hides three things:

1. The 10% return is not guaranteed

Long-term US stock market returns have averaged ~10% nominal, ~7% real, but with massive variance. If you invest from 2000–2010, your annualized return is near zero. The 6.5% from prepayment is risk-free and certain. They're not the same kind of return.

2. Behavior is the variable nobody models

The math assumes you actually invest the $500 every single month for 30 years through bear markets, divorces, job losses, and 2008-style crashes. Most people don't. The average retail investor underperforms the index by 2–4% annually because they sell during downturns. If your real-world investment return is 6%, prepayment quietly wins.

3. Your tax situation flips the math

In the US, mortgage interest may be deductible (if you itemize). Investment gains are taxed (capital gains, dividends). In India, home loan interest gets you up to ₹2L deduction under section 24b. After taxes, the spread between your loan rate and your investment return often shrinks from 3.5% to under 1.5% — which is well within the noise of behavioral underperformance.

The honest framework

Use this checklist instead of arguing in absolutes:

  • Is your loan rate above 8%? Almost always prepay. The risk-free return crushes equity expected returns after taxes and fees.
  • Is your loan rate 5–8%? It depends on your tax situation and your behavior. Run both calculators with realistic returns (try 7% instead of 10%) and see if you can stomach 30-year discipline.
  • Is your loan rate below 5% (rare in 2026, but exists for some refinanced loans)? Usually invest. The spread is wide enough to absorb behavioral leakage.
  • Do you have any high-interest debt (credit card, personal loan)? Pay that off first, before either option. A 22% credit card guaranteed return crushes everything.
  • Do you have an emergency fund? Build 3–6 months of expenses before either prepaying or investing. Liquidity beats optimization.

The hybrid that wins for most people

Stop treating this as binary. Split the $500: $250 to prepayment, $250 to investing. You get most of the interest savings, most of the equity upside, and you sleep better during a crash because half your savings was guaranteed return. The loss versus the "optimal" scenario is small. The behavioral safety is huge.

Bottom line

The "invest because the market beats your loan rate" argument is technically right and practically wrong about half the time. Run the math at realistic returns (7%, not 10%), include your taxes, be honest about whether you'll stay invested through a 40% drawdown — and the prepay-versus-invest gap usually shrinks to a coin flip. When it's a coin flip, do both.
