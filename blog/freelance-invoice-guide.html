How to write your first freelance invoice (with free template)

8 min read · Updated for 2026

You finished the work. You're owed money. Now you have to send a piece of paper that will, somehow, magically result in funds appearing in your account. Most first-time freelancers send something that delays payment by weeks. This guide shows you what an invoice actually needs, why each part matters, and includes a free invoice generator that produces a clean PDF in 60 seconds.

What every freelance invoice must include

Strip the fluff. An invoice is a legal document. Six things, in this order:

  1. The word "Invoice" — at the top, in a font size large enough to read at arm's length. Sounds obvious. Most amateur invoices bury this.
  2. Invoice number — sequential (INV-001, INV-002…). For your own records, for tax, and for clients who pay multiple freelancers.
  3. Issue date and due date — both. "Due in 14 days" is ambiguous; an actual date forces a deadline.
  4. Your details and your client's details — name, address, email. If you're registered for tax (VAT/GST), your tax number too.
  5. Itemized work — description, quantity (hours or units), rate, amount per line. Don't lump it into one big number; clients dispute lump sums.
  6. Total amount and how to pay you — bank details, PayPal email, Wise account, etc. Make this stupidly easy.

The two payment-term mistakes that cost freelancers thousands

Mistake 1: "Net 30" without a date

"Net 30" means "due in 30 days" — but 30 days from when? Date sent? Date received? Date approved? Different clients interpret it differently, and if they want to drag, they'll pick the latest one. Always write an explicit due date: Due: October 15, 2026. Studies of small-business invoicing consistently show that invoices with an explicit calendar due date get paid 1.5–2× faster than ones with "Net X" terms alone.

Mistake 2: No late fee clause

If your invoice doesn't say "1.5% per month on overdue amounts" (or whatever your jurisdiction allows), some clients treat it as an interest-free loan. Adding the line costs you nothing and changes their internal incentive. Many freelancers find that simply mentioning a late fee — even when never enforced — accelerates payment by days.

How to actually get paid faster

  • Send the invoice the same day you finish the work. Not Monday morning. Not "when I have time." That day. Friction kills cash flow.
  • Use the client's preferred currency. If they're in the US, bill in USD even if you're in India. Currency conversion friction is a real psychological blocker for AP departments.
  • Make payment a one-click affair. Add a Stripe payment link, a Wise link, or a PayPal.me URL directly in the invoice notes. The closer to one click, the faster they pay.
  • Number your invoices from a high starting point. If you start at INV-104 instead of INV-001, you look established. This is unfair, but works.
  • Send a polite reminder 3 days before the due date, not after. "Hi — quick reminder that Invoice INV-007 is due Friday, payment details attached." 3 sentences. No apology.

Currency, taxes, and cross-border invoicing

If you're a freelancer billing internationally, three things will trip you up:

  • Tax / VAT / GST — depends entirely on your country and your client's country. As a baseline: most B2B services to clients outside your country are zero-rated or out of scope, but you need to write that on the invoice. When in doubt, write the line item with a 0% tax rate so the field is at least visible.
  • Withholding tax — common in the US, India, and several EU countries. The client deducts a percentage and remits it to their tax authority. You'll need their certificate to claim credit. Ask up front.
  • Receiving payment — Wise, Payoneer, Stripe, and Deel all work for receiving USD/EUR into Indian, Filipino, Brazilian, etc. accounts. Wise typically has the cleanest exchange rates. Bank wire transfers usually have hidden FX spreads of 2–4%.
